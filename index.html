△ The tetrahedron · a verified process

Chain cross-checks into a process you can prove.

One cross-check proves one answer. A real job is several steps — extract, then classify, then reconcile. Veridia chains them: each step is its own cross-check, and each step is bound to the accepted output of the one before it (not just a declared link — you can't swap the input). The whole process gets one composite receipt.

CLEAN — every step's panel agreed. MAJORITY — some step needed a majority. FLAGGED — a step split a field, so the process continues on what was agreed and a human resolves that field. A broken step or a broken seam can't issue a receipt at all. It proves the process was cross-checked and correctly chained — not that the final answer is true.

▣ The cube · a whole department

Roll many verified units into one department you can audit at a glance.

A pipeline proves one process. A real function — accounts payable, onboarding, claims — runs several processes side by side. The cube aggregates them: each unit is an already-verified pipeline or cross-check, and Veridia folds their fingerprints into a single Merkle root — one hash for the whole department. Its honest rule: the department declares the units it expects, so a process that was quietly dropped shows up as INCOMPLETE and is named. A gap can't hide behind a clean-looking summary.

CLEAN — every unit agreed and the charter is met. MAJORITY — a unit needed a majority. FLAGGED — a unit escalated to a human. INCOMPLETE — a declared unit is missing, or an uninvited one turned up. It proves the department is complete, each unit verified, and nothing tampered — not that the combined output is true.

◈ The octahedron · an independent audit

The cube’s dual: don’t trust the department’s summary — check it against the real receipts.

composeDepartment builds a department from real receipts and keeps its own summary of them — name, kind, verdict. verifyDepartmentReceipt then re-checks that summary against itself, which catches tampering but can’t catch a department that lied about a unit from the start, because it never sees the real receipt again. The octahedron is handed the department and the real receipts it claims to summarise, and matches every claim — by hash, never by name — back to the primary source. It also recomputes the Merkle root from the unit hashes, which the department’s own self-check never does at all.

CONFIRMED — every claimed unit matched its real receipt and the Merkle root recomputed clean. UNVERIFIABLE — every checked claim held, but at least one unit’s real receipt was never supplied. DISCREPANT — a claim didn’t match its real receipt, the root didn’t recompute, or the department failed its own self-check. It proves the department told the truth about the evidence it was given — not that the underlying work itself was correct.

In plain English

What's actually happening.

  1. Three independent nodes answer the same task. Independent means genuinely different models — not the same one three times. Veridia checks this first and refuses if they're clones, because clones just echo each other.
  2. Veridia compares them field by field. It normalises away formatting noise (0 and 0.00 and "0" are the same; so are 2023-01-15 and its timestamp form) so it catches real disagreement, not cosmetic difference.
  3. Each field gets a verdict. Unanimous — all three agree. Majority — two agree, the dissenter is recorded, never hidden. Split — no majority, so that field escalates to a human. Fields that agreed still stand.
  4. You get a signed receipt. It binds the three fingerprints (proof they were independent), the chain back to each node's scorecard, the per-field verdicts, and what escalated — tamper-evident and verifiable by anyone, offline.

The honest line: a cross-check proves the panel agreed and was independent — not that it was right. A unanimous panel can be unanimously wrong. Split is where a human belongs. Veridia draws that line; it doesn't pretend to erase it.

Questions

The things people ask.

Why three models, not one?

A single model can be confidently, plausibly wrong — a mis-read total, a wrong-but-valid name — and nothing downstream catches it. Three independent models can: two cross-check the third. Veridia adjudicates their answers field by field.

Why must the three be independent?

Because agreement among identical models is not verification. Three clones tell the same confident lie three times. So Veridia refuses a panel whose members are the same model, and records the three distinct fingerprints in the receipt as proof the cross-check was real.

Does a unanimous verdict mean it's true?

No. It means three independent models agreed — strong evidence, but a unanimous panel can still be unanimously wrong. Veridia proves agreement and independence, not truth. Split fields escalate to a human.

What does the signed receipt prove?

It binds the panel (three distinct fingerprints + the receipt hashes chaining back to each node's scorecard), the per-field verdicts, and what escalated — tamper-evident, Ed25519-signable, re-verifiable offline. It proves the panel was independent and agreed; it does not certify truth.

What's a "department", and how does it catch a missing process?

A department rolls several already-verified units — pipelines and cross-checks — into one auditable whole, folding their fingerprints into a single Merkle root. It declares the units it expects up front, so if a process was quietly dropped the department is marked INCOMPLETE and the missing unit is named. A verified set of units proves each one was checked; only a declared charter proves none is missing.

What does the octahedron (the audit) catch that the department’s own check can’t?

A department’s unit row is a claim about a real receipt, not the receipt itself. If one unit is already the worst in the department, a forged row can lie about a better unit’s verdict without changing the department’s top-level verdict at all — so verifyDepartmentReceipt, which only re-checks the receipt against itself, is fooled. The audit is handed the real receipts and matches every claim back to them by hash, never by name. It also recomputes the Merkle root from the unit hashes, which the department’s own self-check never does.

How does this relate to FallForge Mint?

FallForge Mint is the foundry — it mints one owned, proven, signed model for a task. Veridia is the next step: it takes three of those and cross-checks them so the composition is correct, not just safe. It consumes what the foundry makes; it never touches it.
